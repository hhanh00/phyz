Chapter one. Classical Mechanics.

Quantum theory has no forces and no paths. A quantum particle doesn't follow a definite track. So there's nothing to put into force equals mass times acceleration. But one thing from ordinary mechanics survives the trip into quantum theory. It's a single function built from a system's energy, and it tells the system how to move. This chapter rewrites ordinary mechanics around that function. We'll use a ball, a pendulum and a spring. Then the next chapter can turn that function into quantum mechanics.

We'll describe the same motion three ways, and all three give exactly the same answers. Newton starts from forces. Lagrange scores whole paths with one number built from energy. Hamilton describes the system at one instant, and lets the energy carry it forward. The last step is a rule called the Poisson bracket. That's the piece quantum mechanics replaces.

Forces in, motion out.

Newton's second law says that a force makes a body accelerate. Force equals mass times acceleration. Acceleration is how fast the velocity changes, and velocity is how fast the position changes. So say you know where a body starts, how fast it's moving, and what forces act on it. Then the law fixes everything that happens next.

Take a dropped ball. Near the ground, gravity pulls down with a constant force. So the ball's acceleration is constant too. Its speed grows by nine point eight metres per second each second. And the distance it has fallen grows with the square of the time. Wait twice as long, and it has fallen four times as far.

The rest of the chapter works with energies rather than forces, so let's name them. A moving body has kinetic energy, the energy of motion. It's half the mass times the speed squared. A body can also store energy by where it is. We call that potential energy. A ball's potential energy grows with its height. A spring's is half its stiffness times the square of how far it's stretched.

The force always pushes a body toward lower potential energy, downhill. And the steeper the slope, the harder the push. So the force is minus the slope of the potential energy. For the ball, that's the steady pull of gravity. For the spring, it's a pull back toward the resting position. So Newton's law turns forces into motion, and the force is the downhill slope of the potential energy.

Where forces get awkward.

Newton's law needs every force on the body. That includes forces whose only job is to keep it on track. Take a pendulum, a weight on a string. We call the weight the bob. Gravity pulls on the bob, and so does the string. Gravity is easy. But the string's pull, its tension, isn't known in advance. It adjusts at every moment to whatever keeps the bob on its circle.

For one pendulum, you can dodge the tension. The string always pulls straight along itself. That's at right angles to the bob's motion. So it never speeds the bob up or slows it down. You can look only at forces along the circle.

But hang a second pendulum from the first, and the dodge fails. The lower string now tugs the upper bob at an angle to its motion. So you have to work out that tension before you can do anything else.

We'd like a method that works directly with the angle of each string. It should never mention the strings' pulls at all. Next, we'll build one from energies alone.

One number for a whole path.

Energies alone have to pick out the motion somehow. Conservation of energy isn't enough. It's one equation, and the double pendulum has two angles to find. What works is to change the question.

Don't ask how the body accelerates at each moment. Instead, fix where it is at a start time, and where it is at an end time. Then ask which path it takes between them. Fixing both ends usually pins down the motion just as well as fixing the start and the speed. Say a ball leaves your hand and comes back two seconds later. It can only have been thrown at one speed.

To compare paths, give each one a score. At each moment along the path, take the kinetic energy minus the potential energy. We call that the Lagrangian. Add it up over the whole trip, and you get the path's score. We call that score the action. Every imaginable path gets an action, including paths the body would never take.

Here's the rule that picks the real path out. Bend a path a little, keeping its two ends fixed, and its action changes. On most paths, the change is proportional to the bend, like taking a step on a slope. On the real path, that proportional change is zero. What's left grows only like the square of the bend. Halve the bend, and the change drops to a quarter. So for small bends, the action is flat, like the bottom of a valley.

That's the rule, and we call it the principle of stationary action. People often call it least action. But the flat spot doesn't have to be a minimum. It can also be a hilltop or a saddle.

Let's try it. Throw a ball straight up, and catch it a couple of seconds later. Now imagine other paths that leave your hand and come back at the same moments. But they bulge higher or lower along the way. Work out the action of each one. Bulging the path up or down raises the action by the same small amount. And doubling the bulge makes that rise four times bigger. That's the flat spot. The real path sits at the bottom of the valley.

Turning the rule into an equation.

Comparing paths one at a time can check a guess. But it can't find the real path. For that, we need an equation the real path obeys at every moment. Asking that every small bend leave the action flat gives one. We call it the Euler-Lagrange equation.

It reads as a balance, so let's meet its two sides. Treat speed and position as two separate knobs. Turn just the speed a little, and see how much the Lagrangian changes. For an ordinary body, that's the mass times the speed, which is the momentum. Now nudge the position instead. How much the Lagrangian changes then plays the role of the force.

The equation says the momentum changes at the rate the force sets. That's what Newton said. The difference is that it works in whatever coordinate you choose, a height, an angle, anything. Only the potential energy depends on position. So nudging the position gives minus its slope, which is the force from earlier. For a body moving along a line, the equation gives back exactly Newton's law. For the thrown ball, it says the ball accelerates downward at nine point eight metres per second, every second.

Now describe the pendulum by its angle. We call that angle theta, measured from straight down. The bob's height depends only on the angle. Its speed depends only on how fast the angle changes. So the Lagrangian is written entirely in the angle and its rate of change. The string's pull never appears in it. The Euler-Lagrange equation then gives the pendulum's motion, with no tension anywhere. For the double pendulum, write the Lagrangian in the two angles. You get one equation for each angle, again with no tension. That's the method we asked for.

One more thing drops out. Suppose the Lagrangian doesn't depend on some coordinate at all. Take a body floating freely. Its Lagrangian cares only how fast it moves, not where it is. Then the force term is zero, and the equation says the momentum never changes.

Shifting the system along that coordinate changes nothing. We call that a symmetry. And every such symmetry comes with a quantity that stays fixed. A later chapter states this link in general, as Noether's theorem. Another uses it to get the conservation of electric charge.

A state is position plus momentum.

So far, we've compared whole paths between two fixed ends. Quantum mechanics works differently. It describes a system at one instant, and gives a rule that carries it forward. So we need the classical version of the system at one instant. We call it the state.

Position alone isn't enough. A spring passes through its middle twice in each swing, once going each way. And what happens next is different. So the state also has to say how the system is moving.

We use the momentum for that, the same one that appeared in the Euler-Lagrange equation. We call it the conjugate momentum. For a body moving along a line, it's the ordinary momentum, mass times velocity. For the pendulum, it's an angular momentum. We'll see at the end of the chapter why it's momentum and not plain velocity.

So the state is a pair, position and momentum. Picture it as a point on a map, with position across and momentum up. We call that map of all possible states phase space. As the system moves, its point traces a curve.

The energy moves the state.

Now we want the rule that moves the state forward, written in position and momentum. It comes from one function, called the Hamiltonian. For everything in this chapter, it's the total energy, kinetic plus potential. We just write the kinetic part using momentum instead of speed. Speed is momentum over mass. So the kinetic energy becomes momentum squared over twice the mass.

The Hamiltonian moves the state through two rules, called Hamilton's equations. The first says position changes at a rate set by how the energy depends on momentum. That rate works out to the velocity. The second says momentum changes at a rate set by how the energy depends on position. That works out to the force. Together, they're Newton's law again.

Each rule gives a rate of change straight from the current position and momentum. So knowing the state now fixes the next small step. Then the next, and the whole motion after it.

Let's run it on a spring. Attach a one kilogram mass to a spring of stiffness four newtons per metre. Pull it out one metre and let go. Hamilton's equations say the position swings back and forth like a cosine. The momentum swings too. It's biggest just as the mass passes the middle.

The spring has a natural rate, called its angular frequency. It's the square root of the stiffness over the mass. Here that's the square root of four over one, which is two. A cosine repeats every two pi. So one full swing takes two pi divided by that rate. So the motion repeats every pi seconds. At release, the momentum is zero and the stretch is one metre. The energy is half the stiffness times the stretch squared. That's half of four, times one, which is two joules, and it stays at two joules the whole time. At the ends of the swing it's all potential. In the middle, it's all kinetic.

On the phase-space map, the point goes round and round a closed loop. Every state on the loop has the same energy, two joules. The point just travels around it, while energy passes back and forth between kinetic and potential. Rescale the two axes to the right proportions, and the loop is a circle. The chapter on the harmonic oscillator builds on that picture.

How anything changes.

Hamilton's equations tell us how position and momentum change. But often we want to know how something else changes, such as the energy. And we'd like to know it without solving the whole motion first.

Any such quantity depends on the state. So as the state moves, it changes for two reasons. The position moves, and the momentum moves. Add the two effects, and use Hamilton's equations for the rates. You get one fixed recipe. We call it the Poisson bracket of the quantity with the energy. The rate of change of anything is its Poisson bracket with the energy.

The recipe works for any two quantities. Take how the first changes with position, times how the second changes with momentum. Then subtract how the first changes with momentum, times how the second changes with position.

Here's what it gives. The bracket of position with the energy is the velocity. The bracket of momentum with the energy is the force. That's Hamilton's equations again. The bracket of the energy with itself is zero, so the energy never changes. In general, anything whose bracket with the energy is zero stays constant.

Now one special bracket. Take position with momentum. Nudge the position, and the position moves by exactly that nudge. So its rate is one. The same goes for momentum with momentum. And neither changes with the other. So the bracket is one times one, minus zero times zero. That's one.

It doesn't involve the energy at all. So it's the same for every system, a ball, a pendulum, a spring. Velocity doesn't share this. The bracket of position with velocity is one over the mass. That depends on the mass, so there'd be no single rule shared by every system. That's why we use momentum.

Better still, every other bracket can be worked out from this one. Take any quantity built by adding and multiplying position and momentum. Its bracket reduces, step by step, to the bracket of position with momentum. So when the next chapter builds quantum mechanics, it only has to replace this one relation. Everything else follows.

Where this comes back.

The next chapter, on first quantization, keeps the Hamiltonian as the function that moves the state forward. But it turns position and momentum into a new kind of object, called operators. It replaces the bracket of position with momentum with a quantum version. And the rest of quantum mechanics follows from that one change. Later, the chapter on the harmonic oscillator quantizes this chapter's spring.

The action comes back too. Later chapters apply the Euler-Lagrange equation to fields, with one coordinate at every point of space. And much later, path integrals add up every path, each weighted by its action. They explain why the flat-spot path is the one we see.
